The Asia-Pacific War アジア太平洋戦争

Manchuria to Hiroshima, 1931–1945, told as one connected story

Reading: Ebrey & Walthall, Connections: "World War II," pp. 430–438; Ch. 26, pp. 456–461; Ch. 23, pp. 404, 406, 410–413; Ch. 25, pp. 448–452 Before: Unit 13 · Nationalists vs. Communists Next: Unit 15 · China Under Mao

    Overview

    The unit in one paragraph

    pp. 430, 456, 470

    World War II was fought with indiscriminate bombing of civilians and death tolls in the millions. For East Asia it began in 1931, when Japan's army took Manchuria, and became all-out war on China in 1937. The war in Europe began in 1939; the United States joined after Japan bombed Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941. Japan, a militarized state at home, mobilized its colony Korea and conquered Southeast Asia in the name of liberating Asia, but it could neither defeat China nor match American industry and air power. Firebombing, two atomic bombs, and the Soviet declaration of war came before Japan's surrender in August 1945. The textbook tells this story in a "Connections" chapter, stepping back from the national chapters to show the larger picture.

    Questions historians ask (p. 456)
    • The further the war recedes, the more historians stress continuities across it. The war's impact on individual lives is undeniable; its long-term effect on economy and society is debated.
    • Who was responsible for the war?
    • Was 1930s Japan a fascist state, with an ideology condemning capitalism and individualism, or a militaristic one, in which the military controlled policy? (p. 470)
    The Axis

    Japan, Germany, and to a lesser extent Italy, the aggressors. They shared an ideology but never coordinated military or diplomatic strategy.

    vs.
    The Allies

    The British Commonwealth (including India and Australia), the United States, the Soviet Union, and Chiang Kaishek's China. More diverse, yet their leaders met several times.

    Timeline at a glance

    Part 1

    A world war: who fought and why

    p. 430

    Japan's war began with its invasion of Manchuria in 1931, followed by all-out war against China in 1937. The war in Europe began in 1939, when Hitler's invasion of Poland provoked declarations of war from Britain and France. The United States joined after Pearl Harbor, and Hitler then declared war on the United States.

    Table 1. Why the Axis fought (p. 430; Italy's Ethiopia date from p. 433)
    CountryIts own reasons
    GermanyStill angry at the punitive terms that ended World War I, including lost territory; claimed it needed "living space" for a growing population. Hitler's Nazi Party held Aryans to be a superior race destined to rule. Expanded eastward.
    ItalyFascist; conquered Ethiopia (1936).
    JapanMany believed Japan superior to the rest of Asia and in need of colonies for national security and self-respect.
    Shared ideasNazis, Italian Fascists, and Japan's ultranationalists blamed the social dislocation of the early twentieth century on individualistic liberalism, hedonistic urban culture, and compromising, corrupt politicians. They opposed capitalism and the capitalist powers (Britain, France, the United States), and feared the universal socialism of the Bolshevik revolution as a threat to the national polity.
    Remember

    A common ideology did not make a common plan. The Axis never coordinated military or diplomatic strategy, while the more diverse Allies held several leaders' meetings during the war (p. 430). China's Chiang Kaishek attended the Allied meetings at Cairo and Yalta (p. 451).

    Part 2

    Japan's road to war, 1931–1937

    pp. 456–458

    Military actions and rural crises dominated Japanese politics in the 1930s. In 1931 the army demanded support for its takeover of Manchuria, and the navy attacked the limits on shipbuilding set by the London Naval Treaty. The armed forces made Japan a militaristic state by forcing the Diet to curb free speech and approve war budgets. Few intellectuals dared criticize imperialism; for most citizens being Japanese meant pride in the slogan "Asia for the Asiatics," which papered over Japan's own colonizing. pp. 456–457

    Radical junior officers p. 457

    Young officers heard soldiers' stories of sisters sold into prostitution to save family farms from moneylenders, and read that the conglomerates profited from currency speculation when Japan left the gold standard in 1931. Many studied Kita Ikki's A Plan for the Reorganization of Japan: a "people's emperor" would suspend the constitution, confiscate surplus wealth, manage the economy, and provide welfare; then Japan would liberate Asia. Modeling themselves on the radicals of the Meiji Restoration, the officers wanted to purge the conglomerates, corrupt politicians, and "evil advisers" who kept the emperor's will from the people. They trusted violence to purify the state and distrusted old men.

    Imperial Way faction
    • Hero: General Araki Sadao.
    • Spiritual training: devotion to the emperor, loyalty, self-sacrifice.
    • The Japanese spirit (Yamatodamashii) is enough to overcome material obstacles.
    vs.
    Control faction
    • Battles are won by rational planning.
    • Advanced military technology and sophisticated weapons.
    • General Nagata Tetsuzan, assassinated by an Araki supporter in 1935.

    Figure 2. The two army factions of the 1930s (p. 457).

    February 26, 1936 p. 457

    Junior officers with the slogan "Revere the Emperor, Destroy Traitors" led 1,400 troops to seize the Diet building and army headquarters, kill cabinet ministers, and call on the emperor to proclaim a Shōwa Restoration. Horrified, the emperor had the army suppress them. After a four-day standoff they surrendered and were executed with their mentor Kita Ikki, a victory for the Control faction. (Usually called the February 26 Incident†.)

    The army and the citizenry p. 457

    The military built close ties with the old middle class: shopkeepers, factory owners, teachers, low-ranking officials, and farmers. The army founded the Imperial Military Reserve Association (1910), the Greater Japan Youth Association (1915), and the Greater Japan National Defense Women's Association (1932). Organized at the hamlet level, the Reserve Association took over firefighting, police, road and canal repair, shrine and temple upkeep, emergency relief, and entertainment, and it ran drills, group calisthenics, bayonet contests, and military-approved lectures.

    Social reform pp. 457–458

    Crop failures devastated villages in northeastern Japan. Students sent by Hani Motoko, a journalist and founder of Woman's Friend, reported that farmers knew nothing of hygiene, wasted money on ceremonies, idled, and drank; Hani set up settlement houses teaching poor women sewing, cleanliness, etiquette, nutrition, and thrift. The Agriculture Ministry backed similar plans (social education, cooperatives, economic planning, moral betterment), and a nationwide campaign by the Home Ministry, reformers, educators, and women's leaders pushed thrift to raise the savings rate for emergencies and national projects.

    Part 3

    War in China, 1931–1945

    pp. 430–433; Ch. 25, pp. 448–451

    Unit 13 covered this from the Chinese side in detail; here is the connected outline. See Unit 13, Part 6.

    Manchuria and the slide to full war pp. 431, 450

    1. 11931. Japanese soldiers bomb the South Manchurian Railroad and occupy Shenyang "in self-defense" (the Mukden Incident†). Fear of Soviet expansion, a perceived need for resources, and the belief that Japan's "excess population" needed room drive the army's takeover.
    2. 21932. Japan sets up Manchukuo under Puyi, the last Qing emperor, and encourages Japanese and Korean settlers; by 1945 Manchuria had absorbed about 270,000 Japanese. Anti-Japanese demonstrations and boycotts follow, and Japan's navy bombards civilian quarters of Shanghai before the largest foreign community in China.
    3. 31933. The League of Nations accepts its fact-finding team's report condemning Japan's aggression; Japan withdraws from the League.
    4. 4Mid-1930s. Japan's attempts at a buffer zone in north China fuel student-led national salvation protests from Beijing outward. Even old enemies join in the United Front of 1936.
    5. 5July 1937. The Marco Polo Bridge Incident: Japan invades north China "to protect its interests in Manchuria" and meets fierce opposition.

    Atrocity and "comfort stations" pp. 431–432, 450

    Chinese resistance around Shanghai surprised Japan. When the army took Nanjing in December 1937, spokesmen claimed Chinese troops had shed their uniforms to hide among civilians, "justifying" the murder of civilians. Frustrated that five months of war had brought no decisive victory, officers encouraged their men to loot and rape. Chapter 25 puts the dead at 40,000 to 300,000 civilians and fugitive soldiers and the raped at perhaps 20,000 women; the Connections chapter says "hundreds of thousands" were killed. The foreign press called it the "Rape of Nanking."

    From rape to a system of sexual slavery pp. 431–432

    Shocked by the outcry and worried about discipline and venereal disease, officers expanded the "comfort stations" first launched in Shanghai in 1932. Japanese prostitutes were mostly reserved for officers behind the lines; for front-line soldiers the army took women from its colonies and, after 1940, its conquests. Koreans were 80 percent of the "comfort women," who served troops as far away as Burma, the Dutch East Indies, and the Philippines, in some places up to fifty men a day. Only the war's end freed them.

    A war Japan could not win pp. 432–433, 450

    Geography

    Chiang moved his capital to Chongqing in Sichuan, where near-constant fog hid it from bombers and the narrow Yangzi gorges blocked an overland attack.

    Cities, not countryside

    By the end of 1938 Japan held cities and towns from Manchuria to Guangdong and the rail lines, but the vast land and hostile people meant it never controlled the countryside.

    Guerrillas

    Guerrilla war brought indiscriminate "rural pacification" raids on villages (Chapter 25's "kill all, burn all, loot all"). They failed: 1 million Japanese troops held China's richest regions for eight years without subduing its people or finding a way out.

    People in occupied China tried to stay inconspicuous, but many had to choose between collaboration and resistance. Collaborators set up a provisional government in Beijing in 1937; in 1940 Japan created the Reorganized Government of the Republic of China under Wang Jingwei, a Nationalist who promoted peace with Japan in the name of Greater East Asianism, and in 1943 compelled it to declare war on the United States and Britain. pp. 432–433

    Inside China: two numbers to remember pp. 450–451

    Chinese resistance tied down about 40 percent of Japan's troops even after the Pacific War began. Meanwhile Communist Party membership grew from 40,000 (1937) to about 800,000 (1940), building the base that won the civil war (Unit 13).

    Part 4

    Korea in the war

    p. 433; Ch. 23, pp. 404, 406, 410–413

    The third phase of colonial rule ran from the Manchurian Incident (1931) to 1945. Japan poured capital into heavy industry and infrastructure in Korea to support its wars while crushing the freedoms of the 1920s. In 1936 it declared a policy of forced assimilation: as "children of the emperor," Koreans had to worship at Shinto shrines and pray for his health. After 1937 came military conscription and forced labor, and Koreans had to use Japanese and take Japanese names, in an effort to erase Korean identity. pp. 404, 433

    An economy built for war pp. 406, 412–413, 433

    • Farms: cotton and wool expanded at the expense of grains.
    • Industry: hydroelectric plants on the Yalu powered steel, chemical, and machine factories; a south–north rail trunk line fed goods into Manchuria and, after 1937, China. Mitsui, Mitsubishi, and Sumitomo mined metals for the military. Late in the war, as Japan's own factories were bombed, more plants went up in Manchuria and northern Korea.
    • Korean business: Korean manufacturers profited from Japan's needs, earning the anger of their compatriots.
    • Labor: Korean workers abroad served as far as New Guinea, some as police and guards, and died in battles that made no distinction between combatants and noncombatants. Some young men fled to the hills to escape forced labor and conscription; most were trapped.
    Material culture · A colonial gold mine p. 413

    Japan opened mines in Korea and Manchuria but relied on labor intensification (pickaxes, not bulldozers; manpower, not trucks). The textbook's photo of Korean miners in South Jeolla shows the hierarchy: workers scraping soil and carrying rocks, a foreman, and a manager in a Western hat above, beside the one sign of machinery, a cogwheel. Koreans earned less than Japanese for the same work, and many hid in the hills to avoid the mine draft.

    Koreans on every side pp. 410–412

    Kim Il Sung

    On June 4, 1937, led the most successful guerrilla raid inside Korea, at Bocheonbo near the Yalu. He fought in Manchuria until 1940, when Japan destroyed the Chinese communist armies there, then crossed into Soviet territory and became a major in the new 88th Division of the Soviet Far Eastern Command.

    Other exiles

    Some Korean communists led the Korean Volunteer Corps in China; others joined Mao's units in Yan'an. The non-communist Kim Gu fought with the Nationalists; Rhee Syngman spent two decades in the United States seeking support. Neither side won independence on its own, nor agreed on Korea's future.

    Park Chung-hee

    The future dictator of South Korea (1961–1979) graduated from a military academy in Japan and served as a Japanese army officer in Manchuria against opponents of Japanese rule, including Korean guerrillas.

    Comfort women

    Thousands of Korean women were abducted or lured with false promises of jobs and forced to serve Japanese troops in China, Manchuria, the South Sea islands, and Southeast Asia.

    Part 5

    The war expands, 1939–1942

    pp. 433–434

    Like Italy's conquest of Ethiopia in 1936, Japan's aggression in China drew international outrage but little action. The League and the United States deplored it, yet did not stop it, even though Japanese forces destroyed American property, sank an American warship, and killed American civilians.

    The Soviet front p. 433

    Fearing Japan's aims north of Manchuria, only the Soviet Union gave China significant aid: munitions, airplanes, and advisers to both Communists and Nationalists. More than two hundred Soviet pilots died defending China. In May 1939 Japan's army clashed with Soviet forces at Nomonhan; the fight cost Japan 18,000 men and exposed weak tactics and equipment, and Japan soon sued for peace. Germany and the Soviet Union signed a Non-Aggression Pact in August 1939; Japan and the Soviet Union signed a neutrality pact in April 1941. After Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, Soviet aid to China ended.

    Note: Chapter 25 (p. 451) dates the end of Soviet aid to 1939; the Connections chapter (p. 433) ties it to June 1941.

    Why Japan attacked Pearl Harbor pp. 433–434

    1. 1Pressure. The United States uses sanctions and threats to push Japan out of China: ever-stricter embargoes, and credit for Chiang to buy American arms.
    2. 2Spring 1940. Roosevelt moves the Pacific Fleet to Pearl Harbor to protect shipping and intimidate Japan; Japan's navy sees a threat to its interests in Micronesia and the South Pacific.
    3. 3September 1940. Japan invades North Vietnam to get raw materials, cut supply lines to Chiang, and move toward the oil of the Dutch East Indies. The United States cuts off steel and scrap; Japan joins the Tripartite Pact.
    4. 41940–1941. Unable to win in China yet unwilling to withdraw, Japan opens one front after another, consuming more resources while fearing it will be denied them. It moves into south Vietnam and forces an alliance on Thailand.
    5. 5July 1941. The Americans, British, and Dutch (with the Chinese, the "ABCD encirclement") block Japan's access to oil; the U.S. freezes Japanese assets.
    6. 6December 7, 1941. To secure fuel for the China war, Japan strikes south, bombing Pearl Harbor in hopes of forcing the United States to negotiate. Ten hours later it invades the Philippines, a U.S. colony.

    Lightning conquests p. 434

    Britain expected Singapore to be attacked from the sea; Japanese troops came through the jungle. The same month Singapore fell, President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, putting 110,000 Japanese Americans, more than half of them U.S. citizens, behind barbed wire. Within months Japan took Indonesia, the Philippines, Guam, Wake, the Gilberts, and the Solomons, drove Britain from Burma, closed the Burma Road that supplied Chiang, and threatened India and Australia. With the islands taken from Germany in World War I, Japan had a vast empire, mostly over water.

    Midway, June 1942 p. 434

    Early victories gave Japan unjustified confidence. Six months after Pearl Harbor it aimed at Midway, an atoll west of Hawai'i, to tighten its defensive perimeter, gain a base for attacks on Fiji or Samoa, and force into battle the U.S. carriers that had been absent from Pearl Harbor. The United States had cracked Japan's codes and knew the plan. After defeating Japan's navy decisively, it could focus on Germany; the Pacific War became a war of attrition lasting three more years.

    Part 6

    How the war was waged

    pp. 434–436

    An empire without a plan p. 434

    Japan's empire lacked administrative coherence. Its stated aim everywhere was to make each region and island self-sufficient and to feed Japan's war machine.

    Table 2. Japanese rule in Southeast Asia (p. 434)
    PlaceHow Japan ruled
    Dutch East IndiesDivided among three military commands. Java's grain output plummeted when fields were turned to textile crops.
    PhilippinesA "republic" with a constitution drafted under Japanese supervision, the nationalist José P. Laurel as president, and an alliance putting land and resources at Japan's disposal.
    VietnamInvited in by Vichy France, which collaborated with the Nazis; Japan used French administrators until March 1945, then ousted the French with nationalists' help and kept power itself. Tainted by collaboration, non-communist nationalists lost credibility after the war.
    Malaya, BurmaPatriotic associations, nominally against European colonialism, really to mobilize labor. General Aung San of Burma first collaborated with Japan; British-Indian units left behind formed the Indian National Army to fight for India's independence.
    Liberation, but not the way it was delivered p. 434

    South and Southeast Asians welcomed Japan's message of liberation from colonial rule, not its methods. Like Germany, Japan justified disregard for life by racial superiority: Ishii Shirō (like Joseph Mengele) tortured human test subjects in the name of science. Laborers from Malaya, the Philippines, and the Dutch East Indies, sent to build projects from Micronesia to Thailand, got no food, clothing, or medical care and died of malaria, cholera, maltreatment, and starvation. The Indonesian writer Pramoedya Ananta Toer recalled "unattended corpses" everywhere. Against this stand John Rabe, a German businessman and Nazi who saved thousands of Chinese during the Nanjing massacre, and the Japanese diplomat Sugihara Chiune, whose transit visas saved thousands of Lithuanian Jews.

    The Allied counterattack pp. 435–436

    Japan's arrogance helped its enemies: resistance from China to Indonesia to the Philippines aided the Allies. The British led Indian troops to retake Burma; Australians helped defeat Japan in New Guinea; the U.S. Army under General Douglas MacArthur advanced through the South Pacific back to the Philippines; and the U.S. Navy island-hopped across the Central Pacific. Much of the fighting was in jungles hated by both sides: in the Philippines a third of U.S. troops lay in field hospitals with wounds, dysentery, and malaria, and on New Guinea's Buna–Port Moresby track both sides fought through knee-deep mud against an enemy seldom seen.

    Part 7

    Japan's home front, 1937–1945

    pp. 458–461

    Government ministries were often poorly informed, disorganized, and overextended, and patched together ad hoc measures. The army (watching the Soviet Union) and the navy (watching the United States) competed for resources; civilian ministries duplicated each other's programs. Like Germany, Japan at first refused to mobilize women. Businessmen backed the war as long as their firms survived; citizens cheered the first victories, but shrinking food, higher taxes, and the black market brought misery. p. 458

    Who ran what pp. 458–459

    Figure 13. How each part of the government mobilized society. Choose a row (pp. 458–459).

    Terms to know: New Economic Order (1937), National Mobilization Law (1938), Dōmei News Agency, monpe, Kokutai no hongi (1937), Religious Organizations Law (1939), neighborhood associations, and the Imperial Rule Assistance Association (1944).

    Leadership p. 459

    Japan changed wartime leaders repeatedly: seven prime ministers served between July 7, 1937, and August 15, 1945. General Tōjō Hideki, later executed as a war criminal for declaring war on the United States, was at once prime minister (1941–1944), army minister, home minister, and munitions minister. Yet he never directed naval operations, the navy did not tell him when it fought, the army general staff challenged him, and civilian bureaucrats kept their autonomy. Only the Shōwa emperor received complete information, including plans for the 1941 surprise attacks; fearing replacement, he sanctioned military decisions as his ancestors had.

    Shortages and the labor gap pp. 459–461

    Planning could not overcome shortages. The Zero fighter was built with extreme precision and minimal steel, regardless of the pilot's safety. Lost shipping meant no fuel; bombed factories meant less fertilizer and less food. Malnutrition raised rates of tuberculosis (160,000 deaths in 1942), rickets, and eye disease. Children, then adults, fled cities for the countryside. Conscription emptied factories and mines, and the gap was filled in this order:

    1. 1FirstKoreans were the first to fill the gap.
    2. 2Before 1943Prisoners of war were put to work in steel plants and mines.
    3. 31943 ("finally")Women were allowed to volunteer for work in munitions and aircraft industries.
    4. 4October 1943†University students were drafted when the military ended educational deferments.
    5. 51944Middle school boys started factory work.

    Figure 15. Who replaced the conscripted workers, in the order the textbook gives (p. 460). The textbook does not date the student draft; the end of deferments in October 1943 is from outside sources†.

    By the last months, evacuees burdened villages that then had a meager 1944 harvest. Some people criticized leaders in anonymous graffiti and letters to editors; absenteeism, poor quality, and work stoppages spread. These signs were slight, but enough to make politicians and bureaucrats afraid of social revolution urge an end to the war. About 3 million Japanese had died by then, many in firestorms in the bombed cities. pp. 460–461

    Documents · pp. 460–461

    Excerpts from the diary of Hayashi Toshimasa (1945)

    Hayashi Toshimasa, a Keiō University graduate mobilized as a navy pilot, joined the Special Attack Forces and was killed in action on August 9, 1945. His diary shows the comradeship and resentments of men waiting to die:

    1. April 13. Fellow pilots die one after another, one in a training crash. He thinks of a friend's young widow and asks those left behind to live with pride, their loss an offering for victory.
    2. April 23. After a drinking party he rages at the navy's treatment of reserve officers from the "students mobilized for war" program: he will die for his homeland and his own pride, "absolutely not" for the Imperial Navy. Half his class is already dead.
    3. June 30. A rainy day of ship-recognition slides, records, and bridge. He blames the navy's cliquishness for driving student pilots to want to "die soon."
    4. July 31 and August 9. On sortie day he puts on his best clothes and the thousand-stitch cloth his mother sent, says farewell to his family, imagines entering Hans Christian Andersen's fairyland, and prays for the empire. On August 9 he writes that he will fly a new Ryūsei plane into an enemy carrier.

    Source: Listen to the Voices from the Sea: Writings of the Fallen Japanese Students (University of Scranton Press, 2000), as excerpted on pp. 460–461. The book's questions are in the Review kit.

    Part 8

    Bombing and surrender

    pp. 436–437, 461

    When Japan destroyed U.S. planes on the ground in the Philippines in 1941, it left U.S. troops defenseless from the air; later Japan's troops and cities suffered the same once its air force was gone. The fall of Saipan in 1944, after the navy lost more than 400 planes and almost every Japanese soldier there died, put the home islands in range of U.S. heavy bombers. High-altitude raids did mostly psychological damage, until General Curtis E. LeMay arrived from Europe and pilots perfected carpet bombing: incendiaries dropped at low altitude that burned Japan's wooden cities. p. 436

    Why surrender took so long p. 437

    Even before Okinawa fell, cabinet members urged an end to the war. The army, its troops in China and Manchuria nearly untouched, refused, demanding that all Japanese be ready to die like "shattered jewels" defending the emperor-centered national polity. Recalling Theodore Roosevelt's mediation of the Russo-Japanese War in 1905, the army finally let Japan's ambassador ask Soviet Foreign Minister Molotov for help, but Molotov, unwilling to commit before the Soviet Union was ready to enter the Asian war and busy preparing for the Potsdam Conference, put him off.

    July 26Potsdam Declaration

    Churchill, Truman, and Chiang demand unconditional surrender: occupation, loss of mainland territory and Taiwan, war-crimes trials, and a government of the people's choosing, or "prompt and utter destruction." Stalin did not sign. It does not mention the emperor; the cabinet waits for Soviet mediation.

    August 6Hiroshima

    An atomic bomb, first tested only ten days before the declaration, destroys the city. Children demolishing buildings for firebreaks were among the victims (p. 470).

    August 8Soviet entry

    The Soviet Union declares war on Japan, ending any hope of mediation.

    August 9Nagasaki

    A plutonium bomb is dropped on Nagasaki. Hayashi Toshimasa dies in a special attack the same day.

    August 14The emperor decides

    At a cabinet meeting the emperor instructs the cabinet to surrender, then records a message telling the people they must "bear the unbearable."

    August 15The broadcast

    Despite a plot by junior army officers to steal the recording, it airs at noon. Even amid the destruction, the announcement comes as a shock (p. 461).

    Figure 18. Twenty days from Potsdam to surrender, 1945 (pp. 431, 437, 461, 470). Step through the days.

    A debate that began at once pp. 437–438

    Why did Japan surrender: the bomb, fear of the Russians, or exhaustion? The textbook leaves the question open. Note how the three fit the countdown: the bombs (August 6 and 9), the Soviet entry (August 8), and the exhaustion visible on the home front well before.

    Part 9

    Aftermath

    p. 438

    War crimes and "victor's justice"

    What the trials covered

    Crimes against humanity broadly defined as the decision to wage war; atrocities such as the Bataan death march, the bayoneting of British doctors, nurses, and patients in Singapore, the killing of civilians across Southeast Asia, and the Manila massacre; and the starving, torture, and forced labor of prisoners of war against the Geneva Convention.

    What they left out

    The Dutch in Indonesia convicted Japanese who forced European women to serve troops but ignored cases of Indonesian women; other trials did not mention comfort women. Men of Unit 731, who gave the United States their data on biological-weapons tests on Chinese, were not tried. Allied atrocities were ignored: "victor's justice."

    A new map of Asia

    • The world split into the U.S.-led "free world" and the Soviet-led Communist bloc, with an "iron curtain" (Churchill's phrase) across Europe.
    • Western powers expected to be welcomed back as liberators, forgetting that Japan's victories had exposed their weakness. The United States freed the Philippines (1946) as promised; Britain left Burma and India (1947); the Dutch granted Indonesia independence (1949); France stayed in Vietnam until defeated (1954).
    • China's civil war ended in Communist victory in 1949 (Unit 13).
    • Soviet troops entered Korea in August 1945. To keep all of Korea from falling to the Soviets, the United States won agreement to a line at the 38th parallel, just north of Seoul, and the two powers sponsored two separate states.
    • Japan escaped division. Under U.S. occupation it became a "bulwark against communism" (Unit 17).
    Summary

    Three experiences of one war

    pp. 430–438, 404, 433, 450–453, 470

    Japan launched war in response to domestic problems and what it saw as military needs, spurring institutional and economic development while causing devastation and loss of life (p. 456). The same war meant something different in each country:

    Discussion questions

    Open each for points the reading gives you to work with. The first two are Chapter 26's opening questions; the next three are the textbook's questions on the Hayashi diary; the last four are this guide's own synthesis questions.

    Who was responsible for the war?
    • The army and navy, which forced the Diet to curb speech and fund war (p. 456); radical officers and their violence (p. 457).
    • The emperor, who alone had full information and sanctioned military decisions (p. 459).
    • A citizenry tied to the army through associations and proud of "Asia for the Asiatics" (pp. 456–457).
    • The war-crimes trials blamed the decision to wage war but ignored Allied atrocities ("victor's justice," p. 438).
    What were the war's long-term consequences for Japan's economy and society?
    • Planning moved industry from textiles to heavy and chemical industry and spurred new fields like optics (p. 459).
    • On-the-job training and planning continued; historians stress continuities across 1945 (pp. 456, 459).
    • But about 3 million died and the cities burned (pp. 437, 461).
    Documents: What do soldiers think about as they wait to die?
    • Dead comrades and the wives and fiancées they leave behind (April 13).
    • Anger at the navy; pride; dying for homeland, not institution (April 23).
    • Family, a fairytale afterlife, the empire (July 31).
    Documents: How do soldiers occupy their time?
    • Training and night flying; ship-recognition slides.
    • Beer parties, records, bridge, sleeping in on rainy days.
    • Writing the diary itself, even in the air-raid shelter before the sortie.
    Documents: Why do soldiers fight?
    • For homeland and personal pride, explicitly not for the navy (April 23).
    • Peer pressure and the navy's cliquishness, which pushed students to "want to die soon" (June 30).
    • Loyalty to comrades and family, and the ideology of the empire (July 31; Kokutai no hongi, p. 459).
    Why did Japan attack Pearl Harbor?
    • Stuck in China, it needed oil; the ABCD powers cut it off after the moves into Vietnam (pp. 433–434).
    • It saw the fleet at Pearl Harbor as a threat and hoped a blow would force the U.S. to negotiate (p. 434).
    • Mutual misunderstanding and underestimation (p. 433).
    Why could Japan not defeat China?
    • Distance and terrain: Chongqing's fog and the Yangzi gorges (p. 432).
    • Japan held cities and rails, never the countryside; guerrillas had peasant support (pp. 432, 450).
    • Terror deepened hatred instead of forcing surrender (p. 450); Soviet, then American aid (pp. 433, 451).
    Why did Japan surrender when it did?
    • The atomic bombs (August 6, 9) and the Soviet declaration of war (August 8) came within three days (p. 437).
    • Exhaustion: oil cut off, food short, cities burned, fear of social revolution (pp. 436, 460–461).
    • The army resisted until the emperor intervened (p. 437).
    How did the war shape each country's postwar path?
    • China: the Communists grew in the war and won the civil war (pp. 451–453).
    • Korea: Soviet entry and the 38th parallel created two states; wartime figures like Kim Il Sung and Park Chung-hee led them (pp. 412, 438).
    • Japan: occupied and made a "bulwark against communism" (p. 438).

    From earlier units (review)

    Meiji Restoration
    The 1868 overthrow of the Tokugawa shogunate by young radicals acting in the emperor's name; the model for 1930s junior officers. p. 457; Unit 7, Unit 8
    Kokutai (national polity)
    The idea of Japan as a unique emperor-centered nation; the army demanded all die to protect it. pp. 430, 437, 457; Unit 10
    Kwantung Army
    Japan's army stationed in Manchuria to guard its railroads and interests; it took over Manchuria in 1931–1932. pp. 430, 448; Unit 13
    League of Nations
    The international organization that condemned Japan's aggression in Manchuria without real sanctions; Japan withdrew in 1933. pp. 431, 450; Unit 13
    United Front (1936)
    The second alliance of Nationalists and Communists, this time against Japan, forced on Chiang after the Xi'an Incident. pp. 431, 450; Unit 13
    Chongqing
    Chiang's wartime capital deep in Sichuan, protected by fog and the Yangzi gorges; called "Free China" in the Western press. pp. 432, 450; Unit 13

    A world war

    Axis
    The aggressor powers: Japan, Germany, and to a lesser extent Italy; shared ideology, no coordinated strategy. p. 430
    Allies
    The British Commonwealth (including India and Australia), the United States, the Soviet Union, and Chiang Kaishek's China. p. 430
    Ultranationalists
    Japan's extreme nationalists, who shared with Nazis and Fascists a hostility to liberalism, capitalism, and Bolshevism. p. 430
    Tripartite Pact
    The mutual defense pact Japan, Italy, and Germany signed on September 27, 1940. p. 430Builds on: Axis

    Japan's road to war

    London Naval Treaty
    The treaty limiting naval shipbuilding, which Japan's navy criticized in 1931. p. 456
    "Asia for the Asiatics"
    The popular slogan of Asian solidarity that papered over Japan's own colonizing. p. 457
    Kita Ikki
    Author of A Plan for the Reorganization of Japan, which called for a "people's emperor" to suspend the constitution, seize surplus wealth, and liberate Asia; executed in 1937† after the February 26 revolt. p. 457
    Araki Sadao
    The general admired by junior officers, who promoted spiritual training in loyalty and self-sacrifice. p. 457
    Yamatodamashii
    "Japanese spirit," which Araki believed could overcome mere material obstacles. p. 457
    Imperial Way faction
    Araki's army faction, stressing spiritual training and devotion to the emperor. p. 457Builds on: Araki Sadao, Yamatodamashii
    Control faction
    The rival faction holding that battles are won by rational planning and advanced technology; it won out after 1936. p. 457Builds on: Imperial Way faction
    Shōwa Restoration
    The "restoration" of imperial rule that the February 26, 1936, rebels asked the emperor to proclaim; February 26 Incident† is the event's usual name. p. 457Builds on: Meiji Restoration, Kita Ikki
    Imperial Military Reserve Association
    The army's association (1910), organized by hamlet, that took over community functions and ran drills and lectures. p. 457
    Greater Japan National Defense Women's Association
    The army's women's association (1932), rooted in both cities and villages. p. 457Builds on: Reserve Association
    Hani Motoko
    Journalist and founder of Woman's Friend whose settlement houses taught poor rural women hygiene, nutrition, and thrift. p. 457

    War in China

    Mukden Incident†
    Japanese soldiers' 1931 bombing of the South Manchurian Railroad, the excuse to occupy Shenyang (Mukden). The textbook describes the event; the name is from outside sources. p. 450Builds on: Kwantung Army
    Manchukuo
    "Manchu land," the puppet state Japan set up in Manchuria, treated as a frontier for Japanese and Korean settlers. pp. 431, 450Builds on: Mukden Incident
    Puyi
    The last Qing emperor, installed as nominal head of Manchukuo. p. 431
    Marco Polo Bridge Incident
    The incident near Beijing on July 7, 1937, that Japan used to invade China. pp. 430, 450
    Rape of Nanjing (Nanking)
    Japanese troops' killing, rape, and looting after taking Nanjing in December 1937; estimates in the book range from 40,000–300,000 to "hundreds of thousands" killed. pp. 431, 450Builds on: Marco Polo Bridge
    Comfort stations / comfort women
    Military brothels, begun in Shanghai in 1932 and expanded after Nanjing, staffed by women (80 percent Korean) forced into sexual slavery. pp. 431–432, 411Builds on: Rape of Nanjing
    Rural pacification
    Indiscriminate punitive missions against villages suspected of hiding guerrillas; Chapter 25's "kill all, burn all, loot all." pp. 433, 450
    Greater East Asianism
    The ideal of Asian unity under Japanese leadership used to justify collaboration. p. 433
    Reorganized Government (Wang Jingwei)
    The collaborationist Republic of China Japan created in 1940 under the Nationalist Wang Jingwei; forced to declare war on the U.S. and Britain in 1943. pp. 432–433Builds on: Greater East Asianism

    Korea in the war

    Forced assimilation
    Japan's policy from 1936 requiring Koreans to worship at Shinto shrines, use Japanese, and take Japanese names. pp. 404, 433
    Forced labor
    The drafting of over 600,000 Korean men to mines, harbors, and factories in Japan and Manchuria; over 4 million Koreans worked outside Korea by 1944. pp. 411, 433Builds on: forced assimilation
    Labor intensification
    Raising output with more manpower rather than machinery, as in Korea's colonial gold mines. p. 413
    Bocheonbo raid
    Kim Il Sung's raid on Bocheonbo, south of the Yalu, on June 4, 1937, the most successful guerrilla attack inside Korea. p. 410

    The war expands

    Nomonhan
    The 1939 battle with Soviet forces in western Manchuria that cost Japan 18,000 men and exposed its weaknesses. pp. 430, 433
    Non-Aggression Pact
    The August 1939 pact between Germany and the Soviet Union. p. 433
    Neutrality pact
    The April 1941 pact between Japan and the Soviet Union. pp. 430, 433Builds on: Nomonhan
    Embargo
    A ban on trade; the U.S. embargoed scrap metal and aviation fuel (1940), then froze Japan's assets (1941). pp. 430, 433
    Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere
    Japan's 1940 vision of an Asian economic bloc under its leadership, compared to the Monroe Doctrine. p. 433Builds on: Greater East Asianism
    ABCD encirclement
    Japan's term for the Americans, British, Chinese, and Dutch, whose block on oil it feared. p. 434Builds on: embargo
    Pearl Harbor
    The U.S. Pacific Fleet's base in Hawai'i, which Japan bombed on December 7, 1941, hoping to force negotiations. pp. 430, 434Builds on: ABCD encirclement
    Executive Order 9066
    Roosevelt's 1942 order putting 110,000 Japanese Americans, over half of them citizens, in relocation camps. p. 434
    Burma Road
    The route carrying supplies to Chiang Kaishek, closed by Japan's conquest of Burma. p. 434
    Bataan death march
    The forced march after the U.S. surrender on Bataan (April 1942) in which thousands of American and Filipino soldiers died. pp. 431, 438
    Battle of Midway
    The June 1942 battle, won by the U.S. after cracking Japan's codes, that turned the Pacific War into one of attrition. p. 434

    How the war was waged

    José P. Laurel
    The nationalist Japan chose as president of its Philippine republic. p. 434
    Aung San
    Burmese general who began by collaborating with Japan and later led independence efforts. p. 434
    Indian National Army
    Formed by Indian units left behind when the British retreated, to fight for independence. p. 434
    Ishii Shirō
    Doctor who tortured human test subjects in supposed medical experiments, compared to Joseph Mengele. pp. 434, 438
    Unit 731
    Ishii's unit, which tested biological weapons on Chinese in Manchuria; its men gave the U.S. their data and were not tried. p. 438Builds on: Ishii Shirō
    John Rabe
    German businessman and Nazi who saved thousands of Chinese during the Rape of Nanjing. p. 435
    Sugihara Chiune
    Japanese diplomat whose transit visas saved thousands of Lithuanian Jews fleeing the Nazis. p. 435
    Douglas MacArthur
    U.S. Army general who advanced through the South Pacific back to the Philippines. p. 435
    Island-hopping
    The U.S. Navy's advance across the Central Pacific from island to island (Tarawa, the Marshalls, Saipan). pp. 431, 435
    Zero fighter
    Japan's fighter, the most advanced of its day, built with precision and minimal steel regardless of pilot safety. pp. 436, 459
    Battle of Leyte Gulf
    The October 1944 naval battle in which Japan lost its aircraft carriers, after which kamikaze attacks began. pp. 431, 436
    Kamikaze (Special Attack Forces)
    Pilots ordered to crash into enemy ships, named for the typhoon that repelled the Mongols; with human torpedoes, nearly 5,000 died. pp. 436, 459Builds on: Leyte Gulf

    Japan's home front

    New Economic Order
    The 1937 program to make the empire self-sufficient; it followed the 1931 Major Industries Control Law promoting cartels. p. 459
    National Mobilization Law
    The 1938 law focusing research on chemicals and machines, requiring job training, and rationing labor. p. 459Builds on: New Economic Order
    Dōmei News Agency
    Founded in 1936 by the Communications Ministry to channel news to newspapers; it also gave villages free radios. p. 459
    Monpe
    Baggy pants women had to wear; perms were discouraged as Western and wasteful. p. 459
    Kokutai no hongi
    Cardinal Principles of the National Polity (1937), teaching that the emperor was divine and the Japanese a superior, homogeneous people. p. 459Builds on: kokutai
    Religious Organizations Law
    The 1939 law used to suppress all pacifist new religions. p. 459
    Neighborhood associations
    Local groups that ran air-raid drills and watched for "dangerous thought." p. 459
    Imperial Rule Assistance Association
    The body into which the Home Ministry folded neighborhood and patriotic associations in 1944. p. 459Builds on: neighborhood associations
    Tōjō Hideki
    General and prime minister (1941–1944) who held several ministries at once; executed as a war criminal. p. 459
    Shōwa emperor
    Japan's emperor (reigned 1926–1989†, known abroad as Hirohito†), the only leader fully informed on military plans. pp. 457, 459, 437
    Hayashi Toshimasa
    Keiō graduate and kamikaze pilot whose diary survives; killed August 9, 1945. p. 460Builds on: kamikaze

    The end of the war

    Saipan
    Island in the Marianas whose fall (1944) put Japan's home islands within range of U.S. heavy bombers. pp. 431, 436
    Curtis E. LeMay
    U.S. general who brought low-altitude incendiary bombing to the Pacific. p. 436
    Carpet bombing
    Dropping incendiary bombs at low altitude to burn whole wooden cities. p. 436Builds on: Saipan, LeMay
    Firebombing of Tokyo
    The raid of March 9–10, 1945, whose firestorm killed about 90,000 civilians. pp. 431, 437Builds on: carpet bombing
    Potsdam Declaration
    The July 26, 1945, demand by Churchill, Truman, and Chiang for unconditional surrender, silent on the emperor. p. 437
    Atomic bombs
    The bombs dropped on Hiroshima (August 6) and Nagasaki (August 9, a plutonium bomb), carried from Tinian. pp. 431, 437Builds on: Potsdam Declaration
    Victor's justice
    The later charge that the war-crimes trials judged only the losers and ignored Allied atrocities. p. 438
    38th parallel
    The line just north of Seoul agreed by the U.S. and USSR in 1945, which became the border of two Korean states. p. 438
