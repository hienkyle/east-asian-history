Unit I · Lecture 1

Joseon Korea

The Confucian State, 1392–1800

Ebrey & Walthall, Modern East Asia from 1600, Ch. 15 (pp. 247–264)

Welcome to the first lecture of the course. We begin not with China or Japan but with Korea, under the Joseon (JOE-son) dynasty, which ruled from 1392 to 1910. This lecture covers the dynasty up to 1800.

The textbook frames the chapter around one big process: "extensive Confucianization." Over four centuries, Chinese statecraft and the examination system were copied more closely; the hereditary yangban elite had to devote themselves to education to gain office; and Confucian norms reached all the way down into family life, so that women largely lost the right to inherit. The dynasty also survived invasions by the Japanese and the Manchus, and by the 1700s there were signs of economic growth, social change and new cultural openness (p. 247).

About the name: the founder chose "Joseon" because it was the phrase Chinese sources used for the ancient Korean state (p. 248).

Pronunciation guide used throughout: Joseon (JOE-son), yangban (YANG-bahn), Sejong (SAY-jong), Goryeo (GO-riyo).

Politics: Did the founding of the dynasty bring social change? Why was factionalism so bitter and so bloody? Did the emphasis on Confucian orthodoxy make factionalism worse? Or did the power of the officials, steeped in Confucian ideas, save the country from tyranny?

International: Did Joseon benefit from its tributary relationship with Ming China? Why was Joseon unable to repel Hideyoshi's invasion? How did Korea respond to its first encounter with Christianity?

A few minutes on the background before 1600, since our course starts in 1600 but the chapter starts in 1392.

In the mid-fourteenth century the Goryeo (GO-riyo) dynasty revived after a period of Mongol domination. King Gongmin tried to strengthen royal power: he strengthened the military, expanded tax revenue and promoted Confucianism. After he was assassinated, Confucian reformers allied with a powerful general, Yi Seonggye (E SUNG-geh), who became the force behind the throne. Reforms reclaimed authority over land grants, cancelled tax exemptions for favored yangban, and carried out a national land survey.

In 1389 Yi eliminated his principal rival, and in 1392 he declared a new dynasty, taking the name Joseon. He is known as Taejo (r. 1392–1398). The textbook stresses that this was "in essence a coup from within," a relatively bloodless transition, and that most of the great families of Goryeo survived to play leading roles in Joseon (p. 248).

The other dates on the timeline preview what is coming: Sejong's alphabet in 1443, the birth of permanent factions in 1575, and the Japanese invasion in 1592.

Orient students on the map. (The textbook's own version is Map 15.1, "Joseon Dynasty, 1392–1910," on p. 250.)

King Taejong twice called up 100,000 corvée laborers to build a new capital at Hanyang (Seoul), not far from the old Goryeo capital (p. 248).

Under King Sejong, Joseon's territory was extended north to the Yalu and Tumen rivers. These lands had been settled by Jurchens and other Manchurian groups, so Sejong sent thousands of Koreans from the south as colonists to secure them (p. 248).

Centralization: only in early Joseon did centralization reach the point where magistrates in all of the roughly 300 local districts were appointed by the central government (p. 250).

Point out the places we will meet later: Busan and Tongnae in the southeast (the landing zone for the Japanese in 1592, and later the Japanese trade enclave), Pyeongyang in the north, and Hwaseong (Suwon), where King Jeongjo honored his father in 1795. Tsushima Island, between Korea and Japan, matters for diplomacy: its Sō family managed trade with Korea after 1609.

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 248–249

Taejo's successors included three of the most successful kings in Korean history (p. 248).

Taejong: built the new capital at Hanyang with corvée labor, strengthened the armed forces, confiscated Buddhist temple and monastery property, and created a sound fiscal base for the state.

Sejong (r. 1418–1450), whose record "far outshone any of his successors": improved the army and navy, defeated the Wakō pirates on Tsushima, pushed the border to the Yalu and Tumen rivers, revised land registration, published books on agriculture and sericulture, restored state grain loans to peasants, banned cruel punishments and allowed appeals in death-penalty cases, and added penalties for masters who beat slaves without permission. He also tried, unsuccessfully, to introduce coins and paper money. In 1420 he founded the Hall of Worthies (Jiphyeonjeon) for scholars. His reign produced sundials, an astronomical chart, a new water clock, and a rain gauge in 1442 — two centuries before Europe's first.

Sejo (r. 1455–1468): Sejong's successor died early, and Sejo took the throne from his 12-year-old nephew, which sowed political discord for the rest of the century. He wrote three treatises on military strategy, attacked the Jurchens in 1460 and 1467, set up the Five Guards Command (1466), adopted China's "ever-normal granaries," and ordered the major law code, the Grand Institutes for Governing the State. But he patronized Buddhism, bypassed the state council and abolished the Hall of Worthies, which tarnished his reputation and helped Confucian officials regain power later (pp. 248–249).

Sejong put the Hall of Worthies in charge of inventing an alphabet in 1443 — "the first and only one of its kind in East Asia." His goal was to spread learning beyond the elite yangban. The yangban recognized that it would break their stranglehold on knowledge and therefore opposed it (p. 248). Compare with the textbook's Figure 15.1 (Hangul Chart), which shows vowel pairs, consonants, and how letters stack into syllables. The example on the slide is the first syllable of the word "hangul" itself.

About the image: this is the opening page of the Hunminjeongeum Haerye ("Explanations and Examples of the Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People"), the 1446 book that presented the new script. Note that the explanation itself is written in classical Chinese, the language of the educated elite; the new letters appear in the last columns on the left. Image: public domain, Wikimedia Commons (File:Hunminjeongum.jpg), sourced from Korea's Cultural Heritage Administration. The book's title and date come from the image's catalog record, not from our textbook.

Why it matters for the rest of the lecture: the textbook's summary says hangul "made possible the development of literature in the vernacular language, which could be written and read by those who knew no Chinese, including women" (p. 262). In the eighteenth century women wrote poetry in Korean using hangul, and Lady Hyegyeong's memoirs — one of the outstanding works of the period — were written in hangul (pp. 258–259).

Comparison preview: the "Making Comparisons" box (p. 264) notes that, as with Japan's kana, women predominated among early users of the Korean script, and many more writings by women survive after hangul than before.

Ask: why would an elite whose status depended on mastering classical Chinese be uneasy about a script anyone could learn?

Kings and officials

Exams became the road to office

~30

men passed the exams per year, on average

Birth alone was no longer enough.

But most officials still came from old yangban families.

Officials could check the king through remonstrance.

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 249–252 · Material Culture: Yangban Children's Board Games

Early Joseon saw three trends that might seem contradictory: kings extended central control; committed Neo-Confucians limited the authority of the king, military men and aristocrats; and the yangban kept their political and social dominance (p. 249).

Exams: as in China, the civil service examinations became the main route to high office. Birth alone was no longer enough. But the vast majority of officials came from long-established yangban families, who could best afford education. The yangban protected their advantage: they banned local clerks (hyangni) from promotion into the central bureaucracy, and barred sons of concubines from the exams (p. 250). Only about thirty men a year passed, so many yangban who studied had no chance of office and lived in the countryside (p. 251).

Kings vs. officials: Joseon kings found it hard to exercise their theoretically absolute power because of the "obstruction and remonstrance" of civil officials, almost all from yangban families with longer histories and more prestige than the royal Yi family. Example: Yeonsangun defied the Confucian establishment, purged literati in 1504, cancelled the royal lectures, and was deposed by high officials in 1506. Under his successor Jungjong, the censor Jo Gwangjo pushed a new "recommendation" exam (1515) that his own friends passed; in 1519 the king purged the young censors and executed Jo. Students later saw Jo as a martyr — or he can be seen as a politician "playing the moral card" (pp. 250–251).

Material culture (p. 252): yangban boys learned office titles and career paths through dice "promotion games," where a player could be promoted, cashiered or even poisoned — teaching both the names of offices and the role of chance in a career.

Ask: did the officials' moral authority keep Joseon from becoming a despotism like Ming China, as the textbook asks (p. 250)?

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 250–251, 257

This diagram is a simplification to help students hold the groups the chapter mentions; the textbook does not present it as a formal pyramid.

Yangban: hereditary aristocrats who kept a near monopoly on high office throughout Joseon. Over time the stratum grew and split in two: a national elite of about two dozen top families who supplied a rising share of exam passers, and rural yangban with no hope of office. Some rural yangban formed single-family villages and strengthened their local power; others became, in daily life, indistinguishable from ordinary farmers, though they remained legally distinct (pp. 250–251).

Jungin, "middle people": a new hereditary class in Joseon — clerks, legal specialists, accountants, interpreters and the like, who had lost hope of rising to regular office but wanted to keep these positions for themselves (p. 251). We will meet one example, the interpreter Jeong Myeongsu, shortly.

Commoners: farmers paid taxes in kind or labor and owed military service; commerce was limited to licensed merchants in the capital and peddlers and markets in the countryside (p. 257).

Slaves: pervasive in early Joseon, both private and government. State slaves alone numbered about 350,000 in the late fifteenth century. Slaves made up about 30 percent of the population until 1750 (p. 257). We will look at their decline later in the lecture.

Neo-Confucian debate

Which comes first: principle or force?

Yi Toegye

Principle (i)

Holds all pure virtue, so it comes first

vs.

Yi Yulgok

Psychophysical force (ki)

Toegye's rejection of Wang Yangming set the tone for Korean Confucianism.

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 250–251

By the late sixteenth century, scholars famous for their virtue held high office and debated the metaphysics of Zhu Xi. The question: which of two elements was primary — principle (i in Korean, li in Chinese) or psychophysical force (ki, qi in Chinese)?

Yi Toegye (E TWAE-geh) held that principle came first because it contained all the elements of pure virtue. The younger Yi Yulgok (E YULE-gok) argued that psychophysical force came first, because without it the mind would not exist at all (p. 251).

Context in China: the main challenge to Zhu Xi's orthodoxy there came from Wang Yangming, who downplayed textual study and stressed that everyone can find what is morally right by looking within. Toegye defended Zhu Xi and condemned Wang as heterodox, for leaving moral judgment to emotions "beclouded by ki." Toegye's rejection of Wang set the tone for the rest of the dynasty; people drawn to Wang had to keep their views secret.

Bigger point from the chapter (p. 250): Korean Confucians respected Ming China as a mature Confucian society but did not see Confucian civilization as uniquely Chinese. The most zealous wanted Korea to be a more perfect Confucian society — closer to the classics — than Ming China itself.

Ask: why might a strict orthodoxy make political disagreements more bitter? (This sets up factionalism on the next slide.)

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 252–256 · Northerner/Southerner split: Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, "Namin," "Bugin"

Personal ties — blood, marriage, school friendships, teacher–disciple bonds — had always shaped political groups, but no permanent factions existed until 1575. That year, competition between two men for a key post in the Ministry of Personnel split officials into Easterners and Westerners, named for where their homes were in the capital, not for regions of the country. After 1575 factions became hereditary, and frequent purges prevented unity even in a crisis (p. 252).

Officials justified factions the same way Song Chinese officials had: moral men had to band together against the immoral (p. 253). King Seonjo (r. 1567–1608) disliked factions but made things worse by shifting favor back and forth. In 1589 he accepted a Westerner charge that a rebellion was an Easterner plot and executed more than seventy Easterner officials.

Note on the diagram: the 1575 split and the Westerner split into Patriarchs (Song Siyeol's group) and Disciples after the 1659 mourning dispute come from the textbook (pp. 252, 255). The textbook names the Northerners and Southerners but does not explain where they came from; that link comes from an outside source, the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (Academy of Korean Studies), entries "Namin" (Southerners, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0012113) and "Bugin" (Northerners, by Kim Seong-woo, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0024701).

According to those entries, the Easterners split gradually between about 1587 and 1591. The decisive moment was the 1589 purge that followed the rebellion, and the dispute over how harshly to punish the Westerners, above all the Westerner leader Jeong Cheol. The moderates became the Southerners and the hardliners the Northerners. Southerners were mostly followers of Yi Toegye (Yi Hwang) — the scholar from the previous slide — and included Yu Seongnyong and Kim Seongil. Northerners came from the schools of Jo Sik and Seo Gyeongdeok and included Yi San-hae and Jeong In-hong. After the Japanese invasion, the Northerners split again into Great and Small Northerners, the groups our textbook mentions in the Gwanghaegun story (p. 254). The two entries differ slightly on the start date, so the slide says "c. 1589–1591."

Key consequence for the next slide: in 1591 Seonjo sent a delegation to Japan. The Westerner ambassador warned that Hideyoshi was preparing to invade; Seonjo rejected the warning and did nothing to prepare (pp. 253–254).

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 251–254

Why was Korea so unprepared? (pp. 251–254) - Military decline: yangban without office and rich commoners bribed clerks to be registered as exempt students; local commanders let men off duty for bribes or cloth-tax payments; troop numbers fell. In 1582 Yi Yulgok proposed a 100,000-man army; others called for naval bases and a major army base at Busan. The court acted on none of it, leaving three walled towns — Busan, Tongnae and Kimhae — as the only defense. - Factionalism: Seonjo ignored the Westerner ambassador's 1591 warning. - Japan, by contrast, had fought almost constant civil war since the Ōnin War of 1467. Hideyoshi could mobilize samurai and commoner foot soldiers from all domains, armed with muskets and cannon.

The war: In 1592 Hideyoshi asked Seonjo for free passage through Korea to attack Ming China. When Seonjo refused, Japanese forces overwhelmed the defenders, took Seoul in three weeks and Pyeongyang in two months. Under the tributary system the Ming had a moral obligation to help; despite its own troubles, Ming troops arrived seven months after the invasion, beat the Japanese at Pyeongyang in February 1593, but lost badly north of Seoul later that month and then negotiated a truce. Hideyoshi renewed the fighting in 1597; Japanese forces withdrew only after he died in 1598.

Cost: about 2 million lives lost; farm output took a century to recover to pre-1592 levels (p. 254).

Aftermath: Korea cut relations with Japan, but Tokugawa Ieyasu had no interest in invading, and a 1609 treaty restored relations. Trade was limited to Tongnae, where the Japanese ran an enclave behind a palisade, with a fixed number of ships each year licensed through tallies issued to the Sō family of Tsushima.

The one bright spot in the war, according to the textbook, was the repeated victories of Admiral Yi Sunsin's (E SOON-shin) navy.

His command ship was shaped like a turtle, with a dragon's head at the bow, and was unprecedented in its use of iron cladding. Ten oars on each side let it escape pursuit. Long iron spikes hidden under straw mats kept enemy sailors from boarding. It had a lower deck with six gun ports and an upper deck with twenty-two gun ports on each side, firing shells filled with gunpowder and iron splinters (p. 254).

About the image: a woodblock illustration of a turtle ship from the Collected Works of Admiral Yi (Yi Chungmugong jeonseo), printed in 1795 — about two centuries after the war, so it shows how the ship was imagined later rather than a drawing from 1592. Point out the dragon head at the bow and the oars along the side, which match the textbook's description. Image: public domain, National Diet Library of Japan, via Wikimedia Commons (File:Geobukseon1.jpg). The book title and date come from the image's catalog record, not from our textbook.

Teach source criticism here: the textbook itself calls the record that Yi's twelve ships destroyed four hundred enemy ships in one month "undoubtedly exaggerated." Ask students why such a record might be exaggerated and who would benefit.

Ask the textbook's question: given these naval victories, why was Joseon still unable to repel Hideyoshi? (Answer from the chapter: the army on land had hollowed out, the court ignored warnings because of factionalism, and Japan had a century of battle experience and firearms. Victories at sea did not stop Hideyoshi from landing his armies.)

Between Ming and Manchu

Caught between two giants

YearWhat happened

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 254–255

Just as relations with Japan stabilized, Korea was drawn into the conflict between the declining Ming and the rising Jurchen tribes in Manchuria. In the 1590s Nurhaci united the Jurchens and renamed them Manchus (p. 254).

Gwanghaegun tried neutrality, but most of his officials wanted to back the Ming, whose armies had saved Joseon from Hideyoshi. Factional strife complicated things: Gwanghaegun was a concubine's son; once Seonjo's second queen had a son (Grand Prince Yeongchang), several factions wanted to depose Gwanghaegun because a queen's son took ritual precedence. With Great Northerner support, Gwanghaegun had Yeongchang and his younger brother killed.

1623: the Westerners led a coup, replaced Gwanghaegun with Seonjo's grandson, King Injo, executed or banished the Northerners (who stayed out of high office for two centuries), and reversed his foreign policy to support the Ming.

1627: Injo kept much of his army near the capital to guard against rebels. A 30,000-man Manchu force overran resistance and imposed a treaty. When Injo refused further demands, 120,000 men invaded in 1636. Because the border commander failed to send the beacon signals in time, Injo learned of the invasion only two days before the Manchus reached Hanyang. Surrounded, he had to break with the Ming and enroll as a Qing tributary — seven years before the Manchus conquered China in 1644 (p. 254).

Afterwards: heavy tribute demands; only at the end of the 1600s did Joseon kings drop anti-Qing policies and the Qing lighten demands. Most yangban still despised the Manchus and stayed loyal to the memory of the Ming (p. 255).

Ask the textbook's question: why did the Manchus leave Korea independent but fully conquer China? The book's answer: like the Mongols, they did not see Korea as much of a prize; they wanted tribute for their campaigns, then had too much territory to run; and Joseon proved a nonthreatening vassal that could safely stay autonomous (p. 255).

Biography

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, Biography, p. 253

This is the chapter's Biography feature (p. 253), and it shows a man of humble origins rising through a specialist skill, much like the jungin.

Jeong Myeongsu (JUNG MYUNG-sue) began as a lowly soldier captured by the Manchus in 1619, after the defeat of a Korean army sent to aid the Ming. Over the next decade he learned Manchu and won the trust of Manchu leaders. By the Manchu invasion of 1636, both the Manchus and the Joseon court turned to him; the Joseon court offered him thousands of ounces of silver to help in peace talks.

The resulting treaty forced Joseon to keep a mission headed by the crown prince in the Manchu capital, Shenyang — in effect a royal hostage. During the eight years the Shenyang Residence existed, Jeong was the liaison between the Qing ruler and the Korean crown prince. The Joseon court made him a minister-without-portfolio to win him over. As a middleman he played both sides, profiting from trade and from helping Koreans ransom captured relatives.

In 1639 two Koreans accused him of taking a bribe of 2,600 ounces of silver and seven horse-loads of goods. The Manchus sided with Jeong and sentenced the accusers to death; the crown prince could only get their sentence changed from beheading to hanging. He advised kings Injo and Hyojong on appointments, but fell from power in 1650 after the Manchu leader Dorgon died and a new Korean king came to the throne.

Use the textbook's questions: 1) Why did the Joseon court try to get Jeong to work for them? 2) Why didn't yangban learn Manchu so they could gain this kind of power?

Content note: the Sado story involves murder and violence; flag this before reading the document.

Factionalism after the wars (p. 255): a seemingly minor ritual question in 1659 — how long a royal family member should mourn King Hyojong, Injo's second son — set off purges. Song Siyeol (Westerner) argued for a lesser degree of mourning; the Southerners said this questioned the legitimacy of Hyojong's line. The Westerners split into Song's Patriarchs and the Disciples. King Sukjong (r. 1674–1720) switched sides almost on whim, bringing in Southerners in 1689 and purging them in 1694.

King Yeongjo tried in 1727 to end factional strife with a coalition cabinet of moderates. Radical Disciples rebelled in 1728; he crushed the revolt but learned the danger of threatening yangban interests (p. 256).

Sado: In 1749 Yeongjo made his 15-year-old son prince regent. Fear of his father's ridicule "pushed him over the edge of sanity"; he killed palace staff in fits of rage. In 1762 Yeongjo had him locked in a rice chest in the palace courtyard, where he starved to death after eight days. This split the government into those who backed the king (mostly Patriarchs) and those who sympathized with Sado (mostly Southerners), preparing new purges.

Sado's son became King Jeongjo in 1776. In 1795 he led a procession of six thousand men to his father's burial site, saying it was to celebrate his mother's seventieth birthday. (See the next slide for a 1795 painting of this procession; the textbook has one on p. 256.) Jeongjo wanted to be a model sage king, built a Royal Library, but banned free import of Chinese books he saw as subversive; plotters tried to assassinate him seven times (p. 256).

The document (pp. 258–259): Lady Hyegyeong wrote her memoirs in four parts (1795, 1801, 1802, 1805). Her uncle and brother were executed in 1776 and 1801. In the 1805 memoir she explains Sado's illness as the result of his father's coldness and constant criticism. The textbook's questions: 1) How does Lady Hyegyeong understand her husband's illness? 2) What made it possible for the prince to keep killing people?

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, p. 257

Early Joseon's economy was mostly agrarian with limited commerce. Taxes were paid in kind or labor; most artisans were slaves serving the yangban and the royal house; commerce was restricted to licensed monopoly merchants in the capital and peddlers and fifth-day markets in the countryside (p. 257).

Tax reform after the wars: villages owed a fixed "tribute tax" of local products, but when they stopped making those goods they paid illegal "tribute merchants" to buy them elsewhere — which, ironically, expanded private commerce. The government replaced this with an extra land tax in grain; by 1708 it covered the country and more than doubled landowners' tax burden, which stimulated private, unlicensed merchants. Military service was extended to private slaves for the first time.

Money: in 1650 Kim Yuk brought back Chinese coins; in 1654 the king ordered cash accepted for taxes — the first use of money in about a hundred years. Minting stopped in 1697 because of inflation, causing 33 years of deflation, and resumed in 1731.

Farming: population reached 14 million by 1810 (40% more than in 1650), helped by ending fallowing, converting dry fields to irrigated ones, and transplanting rice seedlings. Yet as late as 1900 average output was about 15 bushels per acre — about China's level in 1400, and about one-third less than Japan and China around 1880.

Markets: more periodic fifth-day markets; Seoul had about 200,000 people. In the 1740s the government stopped prosecuting unlicensed traders, and the 1791 "joint-sales" policy protected monopolies for only six Seoul shops.

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 257–258 · bars drawn to scale within each chart

In early Joseon, both private and government slavery were pervasive; state slaves alone numbered about 350,000 in the late fifteenth century, and attempts to limit private slaveholding went nowhere (p. 257).

As the economy commercialized in the eighteenth century, slavery declined sharply: between 1750 and 1790 slaves fell from about 30 percent to less than 10 percent of the population.

Causes (pp. 257–258): 1. King Yeongjo contributed a little: in 1730 he re-adopted the matrilineal rule for children of mixed slave–commoner marriages. 2. The main cause was the rise in runaway slaves. Rather than pay to chase them, yangban and rich landlords rented land to the growing number of landless peasants. 3. The government replaced official slaves with hired commoner labor: official slaves dropped from 350,000 in 1590 to 60,000 by 1801.

Important nuance: escaped slaves did not necessarily become better off. They now paid rent to landlords, interest on grain loans, and the commoner military cloth tax.

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 255, 258–260 · Image: Jeong Seon, Geumgang jeondo, 1734, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons

The economic and social changes of the 1600s and 1700s came with "a new openness to cultural variety and innovative thinking" (p. 258).

About the image: Jeong Seon (1676–1759), Geumgang jeondo ("Complete View of the Diamond Mountains"), dated 1734. The textbook reproduces a Jeong Seon painting of the Diamond Mountains (Geumgangsan) on p. 255 and notes its "touch of animistic spirits in the swaying mountains." Compare the two in class. Image: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons (File:Jeong Seon-Geumgangjeondo.jpg); the title and date come from the image's catalog record.

Poetry: men wrote in Chinese, women in Korean using hangul. Favorite forms: short sijo (SHE-joe) poems, longer saseol sijo, and the still longer lyrical gasa (GA-sa). Korean poems did not rhyme; they relied on alliteration and cadence.

Fiction: hundreds of tales and novels — by men in classical Chinese, by women in Korean. Many deal with women: some defend womanly virtue, suffering is common, and in some, courtesans steal their lovers' money or humiliate them.

Chunhyang (CHOON-hyang), the most popular tale: a yangban official's son falls in love with a courtesan's daughter who refuses to become a courtesan. They marry secretly at fifteen; he leaves to take the exams and places first. A cruel governor demands she become his concubine; when she refuses he has her beaten and jailed. The husband returns as a secret inspector, removes the governor, and they live happily ever after.

Nine Cloud Dream by Kim Manjung (1637–1692), a Patriarch official twice purged: set in Tang China; a monk dreams a life of Confucian success, high office and two wives and seven concubines, then wakes to see its emptiness. The women are more vivid than the hero.

Political works: The True History of Queen Inhyeon (a Patriarch partisan account) and Lady Hyegyeong's memoirs.

Popular oral culture: rural dances and masked plays were often biting satire. Pansori (PAN-sori), a sung chant with a drummer, spread in the countryside in the 1700s; many songs mocked the yangban, though later Chinese phrases were added to appeal to yangban too (p. 260).

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, p. 260

For much of Joseon, Confucianism was intellectually rigid; in the eighteenth century scholarly circles opened up. The Northern Learning group challenged the prejudice against the Manchus and urged Korea to learn from the Qing to improve the economy and material life. Most came from the minority Southerner or Northerner factions (p. 260).

Hong Daeyong (HONG DAY-young): went with his uncle on a mission to Beijing in 1765 and became interested in Western astronomy and mathematics. He rejected the idea that China was the center of the earth or the only enlightened country, criticized yangban for focusing only on China and neglecting Korea's own history, and attacked their disdain for manual labor and the backwardness of Joseon's economy.

Pak Jiwon (PARK GEE-one): Hong's disciple; recorded his 1780 tribute mission to China. He attacked the yangban monopoly of the exams and satirized them in stories like Master Heo and The Yangban. He demanded an end to discrimination against concubines' sons, freeing official slaves, and land limitation, and urged copying Qing carts and boats to improve transport.

Pak Jega (PARK JAY-gah): a Southerner who studied with Pak Jiwon. In 1786 he proposed inviting missionaries from China to teach Western astronomy and math. His 1788 book Northern Learning urged appointing the best merchants to office and expanding trade with the Qing.

Takeaway: a liberalization of thought, but little effect on policy, because anti-Manchu prejudice and the Confucian bias against merchants were entrenched. Connect to the Manchu slide: most yangban remained loyal to the memory of the Ming.

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 260–261

Christianity reached Korea mainly through books in Chinese, not missionaries — the textbook stresses that in this period no Westerners were allowed into Korea (p. 262).

Some Korean captives taken to Japan during Hideyoshi's invasions converted, with negligible effect. Matteo Ricci's Chinese works on Catholic theology and Western mathematics, astronomy and geography came from China in the early seventeenth century and had more impact. Koreans lumped theology and science together as "Western Learning." They adopted the Western calendar in 1653, nine years after the Qing (p. 260).

Scholars in the 1720s often rejected Christianity: Yi Imyeong (1720) rejected heaven and hell; Sin Hudam (1724) condemned its "selfish" focus on individual salvation, like Buddhism. What drew attention was the pope's 1715 condemnation of Chinese ancestral rites as idolatry, and the Yongzheng emperor's ban on proselytizing.

1784: Yi Seunghun went to China to be baptized but was refused for knowing too little doctrine; he still brought back texts. Several dozen men formed the first congregation at the home of the jungin Kim Beomu, who became Korea's first Christian martyr.

1788: officials asked the king to ban European books; King Jeongjo agreed not to punish Christians but ordered Christian books destroyed. 1791: two Catholics (Southerners) burned the ancestral tablets of one's mother; when they refused to recant, Jeongjo had them beheaded. 1794: the first missionary, the Chinese priest Zhou Wenmo, was discovered and executed, and Southerners suspected of sympathy were demoted or exiled. Jeongjo's attempt at toleration backfired (p. 261).

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 247, 261–262

Joseon Confucians were zealous about remaking Korea as a model Confucian society. Because Korea's original family system was quite different from China's, conforming to the classics required much more radical change in Korea than it ever had in China (p. 261).

Goryeo: the family was neither patriarchal nor patrilineal. Sons-in-law usually lived with their wives' families for years; oldest daughters often stayed home and could continue the family line. Women inherited equal shares, could take property into marriage and keep control of it. Men and women both remarried if widowed. Ancestor worship was uncommon; funerals were Buddhist, with cremation and about 100 days of mourning.

Joseon changes: by the late 1400s yangban observed Confucian mourning (example: Choe Bu, shipwrecked in China in 1488, strictly followed mourning rules for his father). Yangban increasingly adopted nephews rather than let daughters inherit. Widow remarriage declined, partly because a 1474 law barred sons of remarried widows from the exams. By the 1700s Confucian ancestor worship had replaced Buddhist ceremonies and patriarchal domination "reached its zenith." It took about 300 years for Buddhist burial to disappear at the lowest levels of society.

Genealogies: early on, women appeared with brothers in birth order; by the 1700s they were listed after brothers, removed on marriage and listed in the husband's genealogy only as "daughter of" their father, without their personal names.

Why it spread: lower-level yangban with no hope of office used strict family ritual to prove their yangban status, and moral tracts in hangul spread the norms. Differences from China persisted: Korea followed classical rules that discriminated against younger sons and especially sons of concubines, whereas Chinese law gave brothers equal shares (p. 262).

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 262, 264 (Making Comparisons: Women's Situations)

From the "Making Comparisons: Women's Situations" box (p. 264). Compared with Europe in 1700 or 1800, women in China, Korea and Japan were probably more like each other than like women in England or France. In all three, men could take concubines and divorce wives fairly easily, but women could not divorce husbands; wives deferred to men; girls did not go to school with boys; female entertainers were prominent but low in status; and historians tend to see women's situation as declining over time, with a high point between 650 and 1050.

Differences: China's family was patrilineal and patriarchal as far back as we can trace. Korea (Silla, Goryeo) and Japan (Nara, Heian) traced kinship through both sexes and couples often lived with the wife's family. Both grew more patrilineal: in Japan, scholars point to samurai families passing property to a single heir; in Korea, to the yangban's commitment to Confucianism. Neither went as far as China; in Korea maternal kinship still mattered — a yangban's son by a lowborn mother could not take the exams.

Writing: Japan's kana (9th century) and Korea's hangul (15th century) let women write in their own languages, and women predominated among early users. Chinese women, by contrast, chose the elegant classical language over vernacular forms.

Footbinding was not copied by Korea or Japan; widow chastity did become the ideal in Joseon, enforced by the exam ban on sons of remarried widows.

Women's agency (p. 262): elite women faced the most pressure (e.g., avoiding contact with unrelated men), but older women held real power — choosing spouses for their children and supervising daughters-in-law. Women kept religious practices men frowned on: praying to mountains, trees and household gods; reading dreams; hiring (usually female) shamans, mudang, to contact the spirits of dead parents; holding to Buddhist ideas of karma and rebirth. The textbook warns against assuming women were simply submissive — didactic texts, fiction and women's own writing tell different stories.

Back to the big question: why did Joseon last so long?

Ebrey & Walthall, Ch. 15, pp. 262–263 · Next: Lecture 2, The Qing (Ch. 16)

The textbook closes by asking how different Korea was in 1800 than in 1392 (pp. 262–263).

Much had not changed: Korea was still unified under kings of the same dynasty who competed for power with the entrenched yangban elite.

But life had changed dramatically: Buddhism had been pushed to the sidelines by aggressive Confucianization. Although food, houses and clothing stayed distinctly Korean, Confucian campaigns changed basic practices such as where couples lived after marriage and how property passed down. The economy had moved from control over land and people with little money to one where coins circulated, trade was more common, and slavery had declined substantially. The yangban had grown and divided into a national office-holding elite and local yangban with much less power; the national elite split into largely hereditary factions. Intellectually, while the most learned still followed trends in China, it had become more common for leaders to develop ideas in original ways.
